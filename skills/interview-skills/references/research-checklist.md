# Interview Research Checklist

Use this checklist to structure interview preparation briefs. Keep the answer concise unless the user asks for a deep dive.

## Recommended Brief Structure

1. Interview snapshot
   - Company, role, stage, interviewer, date if known
   - User's objective for this round

2. Company background
   - One-sentence company description
   - Core products and business model
   - Customer segments and go-to-market motion
   - Current strategic priorities
   - Main competitors and market position

3. Recent developments
   - News from the last 3-12 months
   - Product launches, partnerships, M&A, fundraising, layoffs, leadership changes, legal/regulatory events
   - Why each item matters for the interview

4. Financial and operating picture
   - Public company: revenue, growth, margins, cash flow, guidance, segment trends, and key operating metrics from filings or earnings materials
   - Private company: funding, headcount signals, customer wins, pricing, product adoption, public traction, or credible press
   - Summarize direction of travel rather than dumping numbers

5. Culture and leadership
   - Mission, values, operating principles, and repeated language from official sources
   - Founder and leadership background when relevant
   - Possible culture signals from hiring pages, blogs, interviews, and employee-facing material
   - Treat Glassdoor or forum content as anecdotal and label it accordingly

6. Role analysis
   - What the role appears responsible for
   - Skills and traits likely being evaluated
   - Why the role may exist now
   - How the role connects to company strategy, product roadmap, revenue, customers, or internal operations

7. Role-specific external context
   - Industry trends affecting the function
   - Tooling, regulation, business model, customer behavior, or competitive shifts relevant to the role
   - Recent articles, standards, or public discussions that a strong candidate should know

8. Interviewer background
   - Public professional history
   - Current scope and likely interview angle
   - Public work, talks, writing, patents, code, publications, or investments when relevant
   - Avoid protected traits, private contact information, family details, home location, or non-professional speculation

9. Candidate positioning
   - Best-fit experiences from the user's background
   - STAR stories or portfolio examples to prepare
   - Likely objections or gaps to address
   - One concise answer to "Why this company?" and "Why this role?"

10. Questions to ask
   - Company strategy questions
   - Role success and ramp questions
   - Team and operating cadence questions
   - Interviewer-specific questions
   - Avoid questions easily answered by the first page of the company website

## Must-Know List Before the First Round

The user should be able to speak these five items from memory, without notes. Treat them as a self-check before walking into round one:

1. **CEO**: who they are, their background, and how they talk about the company
2. **Recent deals**: for banking, investing, or public-company roles, recent IPOs, M&A, and debt issuances, landmark deals, and 3–5 deals in the user's industry of interest
3. **Stock performance**: for public companies, recent share-price movement and its main drivers
4. **Core values**: the company's stated values and operating principles
5. **Differentiated advantage**: what the company does that competitors cannot easily copy

Adapt by role. Deals and stock performance fit finance tracks and public companies; for other roles or private companies, use equivalents such as funding rounds, product launches, and business momentum signals. Verify each item against current sources and label anything that cannot be confirmed.

## Source Quality Labels

Use source labels when summarizing evidence:

- Confirmed: official company, filing, investor, regulatory, or direct public profile source
- Reported: reputable press or trade publication
- Anecdotal: forum, review site, social media, or unverified individual account
- Inferred: reasoned conclusion from multiple facts; state the basis

## Question Patterns

A strong question is context first, question second: two or three sentences of setup that cite what was actually found, then one focused question. The setup shows the interviewer the user's reasoning, demonstrates homework, and signals genuine interest. Avoid naked open-ended questions ("How do you see the market?"), hollow one-liners, and macro questions disconnected from the interviewer's own work.

Worked example, from interview-coaching material: a candidate read the interviewer's Bloomberg profile and noticed that an ETF tracking China A-shares used a synthetic derivative underlying instead of real stocks. In the interview she described what she had read and asked why the product was structured that way. A scheduled 15-minute conversation turned into a 30-minute discussion of product design, client preference, and tracking error, and she received the offer. Treat this story as an anecdotal illustration of the method, not as verified evidence about any firm.

Company strategy:

```text
I noticed [recent development]. How is that changing priorities for this team over the next 6-12 months?
```

Role success:

```text
What would make someone in this role clearly successful after the first 90 days?
```

Interviewer-specific:

```text
I saw you worked on [public professional topic]. How does that experience shape what you look for in this role?
```

Career transition:

```text
I saw on LinkedIn that you moved from [group or firm] to [group or firm]. What changed in the work you do, and how did that transition go?
```

Deal or business detail:

```text
I read your interview in [publication] about [specific deal or product]. I noticed [specific detail]. What drove that choice?
```

Culture:

```text
Your materials emphasize [value or operating principle]. What does that look like in a hard tradeoff?
```

Financial or business model:

```text
The recent numbers suggest [trend]. What is the team most focused on improving from here?
```
